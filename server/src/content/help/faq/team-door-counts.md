Open the **Timeline**. Above the canvasser table there's a **by-team** breakdown: each team's people, doors, survey doors, voters surveyed and connection rate — plus a **Campaign** row underneath.

That last row matters. The teams **add up to the campaign total**, so you can read one team's number to a client and know it's consistent with the number you'd quote for the whole campaign. Click any team to drill into just their canvassers.

## What "doors" means

**One door = one house, in one round.** If two canvassers knock the same house in the same round, that's **one** door, not two — the second knock was almost certainly a mistake, and you shouldn't bill for it. If you deliberately go back in a **new round**, that's a new door.

This is why the "Doors" number is a little lower than the raw count of everything everyone recorded, and the line under the table explains the difference whenever there is one.

## People who left the team

**Their doors still count, and they still count toward their team.** Somebody who knocked for you and then quit — whose account you deactivated, or who you removed from the campaign — keeps their doors on the team they were on. You don't have to do anything.

The team is recorded on each door **at the moment it's knocked**, so nothing you do to someone's account afterwards can move their work to a different team, or lose it.

## "Knocked by two different teams"

If you ever see that note under the by-team table, two teams have worked some of the same houses. Each team is credited for the work they actually did, and the campaign still counts the house once — so the numbers stay honest. But it usually means two walk lists overlapped, and it's worth a look.

## Surveys: doors vs voters

You'll see **two** survey numbers, because they answer different questions:

- **Survey doors** — houses where at least one survey was taken. This is what the **connection rate** divides by.
- **Voters surveyed** — people surveyed. One house can have several voters, so this is usually the higher number.

Neither is wrong; they're just counting different things.
